TLS (Transport Layer Security) encrypts a connection and proves that the server at the other end owns the name you asked for. The proof rests on a **public key infrastructure** (PKI): certificates, the certificate authorities (CAs) that sign them, and the lists of authorities that every operating system and browser trusts. Every HTTPS request your monitor makes or answers depends on it, and an expired or misissued certificate takes a site down as surely as a crashed server.

## What It Is

### Keys and Signatures

**Public-key cryptography** works with a key pair. The **private key** stays secret with its owner. The **public key** goes to anyone. Data signed with the private key verifies with the public key, and nobody without the private key can produce a valid signature. That is the whole basis of a certificate: a trusted party signs a statement that a public key belongs to a name.

In TLS 1.3, the certificate key only signs. During the handshake, both sides create a fresh key pair for this one connection and combine them in a **key exchange** to agree on a session key. The server signs the handshake with its certificate key, in the **CertificateVerify** message, to prove it holds the private key. Because the session key comes from throwaway keys, a stolen certificate key later cannot decrypt traffic recorded today. This property is **forward secrecy**. The <TrackLink slug="networking">Networking track</TrackLink> and <WeekLink n={4} anchor="tls-making-the-connection-private">Week 4</WeekLink> walk through the handshake itself.

### What a Certificate Holds

A certificate is a small file in the **X.509** format, spelled `x509` in the name of the Go package and in its error messages. Its fields:

- **Subject**: who the certificate is for. For a CA, its name.
- **Subject alternative names** (SANs): the DNS names the certificate covers, such as `status.example.com`, or IP addresses for a certificate issued to an address. Clients compare the host name against this list only. Go has ignored the older common name field for host names since Go 1.15.
- **Public key**: the key the server proves it holds.
- **Issuer**: the name of the CA that signed it.
- **Validity**: `NotBefore` and `NotAfter`. Outside those dates, clients reject it.
- **Serial number**: unique per CA, which names one certificate in a revocation.
- **Key usage** and **extended key usage**: what the key can do. A server certificate carries `serverAuth`. A CA certificate carries `certSign`.
- **Basic constraints**: whether the certificate belongs to a CA, and how many CAs can sit below it.
- **Signature**: the issuer signature over all of the above.

Certificates travel in **PEM** files, Privacy-Enhanced Mail in full, a name left over from its first use: base64 text between `-----BEGIN CERTIFICATE-----` and `-----END CERTIFICATE-----` lines. `openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -text` prints every field.

### The Chain of Trust

A **certificate authority** is an organization that checks who controls a name and signs certificates for it. Your operating system and your browser ship a **trust store**: the root certificates they accept, about 150 of them from a few dozen CAs. Go reads the trust store of the system: on Linux, a file such as `/etc/ssl/certs/ca-certificates.crt`; on macOS and Windows, it asks the platform verifier.

A **root certificate** signs itself, and only its presence in the trust store makes it trusted. Its private key is too valuable to keep online: the CA keeps it in a hardware security module in a locked room and uses it a few times a year, to sign an **intermediate certificate**. The intermediate signs the **leaf**, the certificate your server uses. When an intermediate key leaks, the CA revokes that intermediate and keeps the root.

The server sends its leaf plus the intermediates, not the root. The client builds a path from the leaf, through each issuer, to a root in its own store, and checks every signature on the way. A server that forgets the intermediate works in browsers that cache or fetch intermediates and fails in `curl` and Go, which do neither.

### What a Client Checks

For every connection, a TLS client checks:

1. **The name.** The host it dialed appears in the SANs, with a wildcard such as `*.example.com` matching one label only.
2. **The dates.** The current time lies between `NotBefore` and `NotAfter`, by the clock of the client. A wrong clock breaks every certificate.
3. **The chain.** Each signature verifies with the public key of the next certificate up, and the path ends at a trusted root.
4. **The usage.** Each CA certificate in the path has the right to sign certificates, and the leaf has the right to authenticate a server.

In Go, each failure has its own type: `x509.HostnameError`, `x509.CertificateInvalidError` with the reason `x509.Expired`, and `x509.UnknownAuthorityError`. Your monitor turns each into a message a person can act on.

### Revocation and Short Lifetimes

A CA **revokes** a certificate when its key leaks or when the CA finds that it issued the certificate by mistake. Clients learn about revocations from **certificate revocation lists** (CRLs), which the CA publishes, or from **OCSP**, the Online Certificate Status Protocol, which answers for one certificate at a time. Neither works well in practice: browsers fetch CRLs in compressed batches, and an OCSP check leaks which sites you visit. Let's Encrypt stopped running OCSP in 2025 and publishes CRLs only.

The industry answer is short lifetimes. A stolen key is useful only until the certificate expires. The CA/Browser Forum, where CAs and browser makers set the rules, cut the maximum lifetime of a public certificate from 398 days to 200 days in March 2026, and lowers it to 100 days in March 2027 and to 47 days in March 2029. Let's Encrypt issues 90-day certificates today, and moves to 64 days in February 2027 and 45 days in 2028. At 45 days, a person renewing by hand renews eight times a year per site, and misses one. Automation is the only option.

### ACME and Certificate Transparency

**ACME**, the Automatic Certificate Management Environment protocol, automates issuance. Request for Comments (RFC) 8555, the Internet standard for ACME, defines it. An ACME client creates an **account** with a key pair of its own and signs every request with that account key. It places an **order** for a list of names. For each name, the CA returns an **authorization** with one or more **challenges**, ways to prove control:

- **HTTP-01**: serve a token at `http://<name>/.well-known/acme-challenge/<token>`, on port `80`. It cannot issue a wildcard certificate.
- **DNS-01**: publish a token in a TXT record at `_acme-challenge.<name>`. It works for wildcards and for servers that the internet cannot reach, and it needs an API key for your DNS provider.
- **TLS-ALPN-01**: answer a special TLS handshake on port `443`.

Once every authorization is valid, the client sends a **certificate signing request** (CSR): its new public key and the names, signed with the matching private key. The CA signs the certificate, and the client downloads it with the chain.

Every certificate a public CA issues goes into public, append-only **Certificate Transparency** (CT) logs, and Chrome, Safari and Firefox reject a certificate without proof of logging. Anyone can search the logs at `crt.sh`, which means a CA that misissues a certificate for your domain leaves evidence you can find. It also means every name you put in a certificate is public.

### Private PKI and Mutual TLS

Not every certificate needs a public CA. A company runs its own **private CA** for internal services, and installs its root on its own machines only. With **mutual TLS** (mTLS), the client also presents a certificate, and the server checks it against the private CA: each service proves its identity to the other. Service meshes such as Linkerd issue short-lived certificates to every pod this way. For local development, `mkcert` creates a private CA, adds it to the trust store of your laptop and issues certificates for `localhost`.

## Common Mistakes

- **Turning off verification.** `InsecureSkipVerify: true` in Go, or `curl -k`, accepts any certificate, including one from an attacker in the middle. It also hides the real error. Fix the trust instead: add your private root to `RootCAs`, or fix the chain on the server.
- **A missing intermediate.** The server sends only the leaf. Chrome works because it fetched the intermediate before, and Go and `curl` fail with an unknown authority error. Serve the full chain file, which cert-manager and most ACME clients write for you.
- **Renewal that nobody watches.** The certificate renews by itself until the day someone closes port `80` or moves the DNS record. Then it expires within 30 days. Alert on days left, from outside the server.
- **A wrong clock.** A machine whose clock is a day off rejects valid certificates and accepts expired ones. Run NTP, the Network Time Protocol, on every server. The <TrackLink slug="time-ntp">Time and NTP track</TrackLink> covers why.
- **Burning Let's Encrypt rate limits.** Five failed validations per name per hour, and five certificates for the same names per week. Get everything working against the staging environment first.
- **Private keys in Git or in images.** A key in a repository is public for good, even after you delete the commit. Keep keys in Kubernetes Secrets or a secret manager, and issue a new certificate when a key leaks.

## Going Deeper

- **The TLS 1.3 handshake in detail.** RFC 8446 explains the key schedule, session resumption and early data, which saves a round trip at the price of a replay risk.
- **DNS-01 and wildcard certificates.** Needed for names that the internet cannot reach and for `*.example.com`, at the price of a DNS API key on your server.
- **Certificate Transparency monitoring.** Services watch the CT logs and alert you when any CA issues a certificate for your domain.
- **Post-quantum key exchange.** Go 1.24 enables the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key exchange by default, which protects recorded traffic from future quantum computers.
