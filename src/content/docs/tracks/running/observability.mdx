A program in production gives you no debugger and no breakpoints. You get only what it writes down while it runs. **Observability** is how well you can answer a new question about a running system from that output alone, without shipping new code first. This page covers the four kinds of output a service gives off, how Prometheus collects metrics, and how to instrument a Go handler with counts that stay correct and cheap to keep.

## What It Is

### Monitoring and Observability

**Monitoring** answers questions you asked in advance: is the api up, is the error rate above 1%. You write a check or an alert for each one. Observability covers the questions you did not think of before the incident: why only requests for one monitor are slow, why the queue grows only after 02:00. Monitoring needs a few numbers. Observability needs output rich enough to slice by a field you choose during the incident, such as a route, a monitor ID or a status code.

Two views complement each other. A **black-box** check sees the service from outside, the way a user does: your uptime monitor itself is a black-box check of other sites. A **white-box** check reads numbers from inside the program: request counts, queue depth, time spent in SQL. A black-box check tells you that users suffer. A white-box metric tells you why.

### Four Signals

Each type of output, called a **signal**, answers a different question:

- A **log** line records one event, such as one request answered or one login refused. Logs answer what happened to this one thing.
- A **metric** is a number measured over time, such as requests per second or jobs in the queue. Metrics answer how much and how fast for the whole system, and their cost stays flat whatever the traffic.
- A **trace** follows one unit of work, such as a request or a job, through every program it touches. Each timed step is a **span**. Traces answer where the time goes.
- A **profile** records where a process spends its CPU time or its memory, down to the function. Profiles answer which code is responsible for the cost.

The signals hand you from one to the next. A metric shows that the 95th-percentile latency of one route doubled at 14:02. An **exemplar**, a trace ID stored next to one histogram observation, leads from that graph to one slow request on the route. Its trace shows that the time goes to one SQL query. The log lines that carry the same trace ID show the monitor ID and the error. When the trace shows the time inside the program instead, a CPU profile names the function that spends it.

Profiles differ from the other three: a program does not write them on every request. In Go, the `net/http/pprof` package serves them on demand. On a request to `/debug/pprof/profile?seconds=10`, the Go runtime interrupts the program 100 times a second for 10 seconds, notes the function each thread runs at that instant, and returns the totals. **Continuous profiling** tools, such as Grafana Pyroscope and Parca, collect a profile from every process on a schedule and keep it. With those profiles, you compare where CPU time goes before and after a deploy.

### How Prometheus Collects Metrics

Prometheus **pulls** metrics. Your program keeps its current counts in memory and serves them as text on an HTTP path, `/metrics` by convention. Each program Prometheus reads from is a **target**. On every **scrape interval**, 15 seconds in the uptime monitor, Prometheus sends `GET /metrics` to each target and stores every number in the answer with the time of the scrape.

{/* prose-allow: sample */}
One stored pair of timestamp and value is a **sample**. All the values of one metric with one set of label values form one **time series**. Prometheus also writes a series of its own per target, `up`: `1` when the scrape succeeds, `0` when it fails. An alert on `up == 0` catches a program that stops, which no metric of that program can report.

Pulling has two consequences. Prometheus knows which targets exist and notices a silent one at the next scrape. And a program does not need to know where Prometheus runs: it only answers requests. A job that runs for a few seconds and exits finishes before the next scrape, which is why batch jobs push their final numbers to a **Pushgateway** that Prometheus then scrapes.

### Counters, Gauges and Histograms

Three metric types cover most needs:

- A **counter** only goes up, and starts again at zero when the process restarts. Its raw value means little; its rate is the signal. The PromQL function `rate(http_requests_total[5m])` gives the increase per second over the last five minutes and accounts for restarts. PromQL is the Prometheus query language.
- A **gauge** goes up and down and holds a current value, such as jobs in the queue.
- A **histogram** counts observations, such as request durations, into **buckets** by upper bound. From the buckets, `histogram_quantile` estimates a **percentile**: the 95th percentile, or p95, is the duration that 95% of requests stay under.

**Labels** are key-value pairs that split a metric into series: `http_requests_total{handler="status",code="200"}` and `{handler="status",code="500"}` are two series of one metric. Every distinct combination of label values is one more series that Prometheus keeps in memory. The number of series of a metric is its **cardinality**. Label only with values from a short, fixed list: handler names, status codes, results. Never label with a user ID, a URL path or an error message.

A histogram costs more series than it looks. With the default buckets of `client_golang`, eleven bounds from 5 ms to 10 seconds, one histogram series exposes twelve `_bucket` series, the extra one for `+Inf`, plus `_sum` and `_count`: fourteen series per label set.

### What to Measure

Two short lists keep you from measuring everything and understanding nothing. For anything that answers requests, the **RED method** names three metrics: the **rate** of requests, the **errors** among them, and the **duration** of each. For a resource, such as a disk, a connection pool or a queue, the **USE method** names **utilization**, how busy it is, **saturation**, how much work waits for it, and **errors**. The Google book Site Reliability Engineering names latency, traffic, errors and saturation as the **four golden signals**, which match RED plus saturation.

Each bucket carries an `le` label, short for less than or equal, that holds its upper bound. Buckets are cumulative: an observation of 0.04 seconds increments every bucket with a bound of 0.05 or more. Each bucket count is a counter, which makes `rate` work on it, and `histogram_quantile` reads the percentile between two bounds by straight-line interpolation. A p95 can only be as precise as the buckets around it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Labeling with user input.** A `path` label makes one series per monitor ID and one per path a scanner tries, and Prometheus keeps each in memory for hours. Prometheus memory grows until the kernel ends the process for running out of memory. Label with the route pattern or a fixed handler name, and collapse unknown values into one, such as `unmatched`.
- **Alerting on an average.** Nineteen requests of 10 ms and one of 3 seconds average 160 ms, which looks healthy while one user in twenty waits 3 seconds. Alert on a percentile from a histogram, or on the share of requests over a threshold.
- **Averaging percentiles.** The average of the p95 of three api replicas is not the p95 of the service. Sum the bucket rates across replicas first, then call `histogram_quantile` once.
- **Buckets that miss the interesting range.** With bounds at 5 and 10 seconds, a check that times out at 5 seconds lands anywhere in between. Put bucket bounds next to the durations you need to tell apart, such as your timeout and your latency target.
- **Series that appear only on the first error.** A `code="500"` series that does not exist yet has no earlier value, and `rate` over it shows nothing for the first failure. Create the series you alert on at zero at start, as the Week 13 worker does with `up`, `down` and `error`.
- **Using `rate` on a gauge or reading a raw counter.** `rate` assumes a counter: every drop looks like a restart. A raw counter shows the total since the last restart, which says nothing about now.
- **Serving `/metrics` on the public port.** It lists your routes and their traffic. Serve it on a second, internal port, which also keeps the scrapes out of your own request counts.

## Going Deeper

- **OpenTelemetry metrics and the collector.** OpenTelemetry carries metrics too, and its collector converts between the OpenTelemetry Protocol and the Prometheus format, which matters once a vendor or a second team receives your data.
- **Exemplars.** Attaching the trace ID to histogram observations links a spike on a graph straight to one slow trace.
- **Native histograms.** Prometheus can choose high-resolution buckets itself, which removes the guesswork of fixed bounds and the cost of one series per bucket.
- **Log aggregation with Grafana Loki.** Loki indexes only the labels of each log stream, not the text, which keeps it cheap next to Prometheus.
- **Tail-based sampling.** The collector can wait until a trace ends and keep every slow or failed trace plus a share of the rest, instead of deciding at the first span.
- **Long-term storage.** One Prometheus keeps its data on one disk for a limited time. Thanos and Grafana Mimir store metrics in object storage for months and query across many Prometheus servers.
