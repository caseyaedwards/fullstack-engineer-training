Your monitor can already tell you that a site is slow. This week it learns to tell you _where_ the time went.

Between your code asking for `https://example.com` and the first byte of the answer arriving, four things happen in order. A DNS (Domain Name System) lookup turns the name into an IP (Internet Protocol) address. A TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) handshake opens a connection. A TLS (Transport Layer Security) handshake makes that connection private. Then the server does its work and replies. You'll learn what each step does, then make your monitor time every one of them.

## Goals

- Explain how a name like `example.com` becomes an IP address, hop by hop.
- Explain what the TCP and TLS handshakes do, and why each one costs a round trip.
- Time DNS, connect, TLS and first byte on every check, with Go's `net/http/httptrace` package.
- Read those timings to tell where a slow or failing request got stuck.

## Concepts

### Addresses, ports and round trips

Machines on the internet find each other by **IP address** (Internet Protocol address). An IPv4 address looks like `203.0.113.10`; the newer IPv6 kind looks like `2001:db8::10`. A **port** picks out one program on that machine. Web servers listen on port 80 for HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol, the way browsers and servers talk) and on port 443 for HTTPS, its secure version.

A URL (web address) such as `https://example.com/` tells you the port, through the `https`, but not the address. Finding the address is the first job.

One more idea runs through this whole week. A **round trip** is one message sent and its reply received. The time it takes, the **round-trip time** or RTT, depends mostly on distance: a few milliseconds (ms) across a city, 70 to 150 ms across an ocean. Most of what you'll measure this week is a count of round trips.

:::note
The `curl`, `dig` and CLI outputs on this page are representative examples, not captured from one real run, and their addresses come from `203.0.113.0/24`, a range reserved for documentation. When you run the commands yourself, you'll see real addresses, different times and a few extra lines.
:::

### DNS: from a name to an address

DNS, the Domain Name System, is a database spread over thousands of servers that maps names to records. The record you need most is the **A record**, which holds an IPv4 address. An AAAA record holds an IPv6 one.

Names are read right to left, as a tree. At the top is the root, written as a dot. Below it are the top-level domains (TLDs) such as `com` and `org`. Below `com` is `example`. Different organisations run each level, and each level only knows who is responsible for the level below it. That's why a lookup can take several hops.

Five roles take part:

- The **stub resolver** is the small DNS client built into your operating system (or into Go). It asks one question and waits for a complete answer.
- The **recursive resolver** does the legwork for the stub. It might be your home router, your internet provider's server, or a public one such as `1.1.1.1` or `8.8.8.8`. It remembers (caches) every answer it gets.
- The **root servers** know where to find the server for every TLD.
- The **TLD servers** for `.com` know which servers hold the records for each `.com` domain.
- The **authoritative server** for `example.com` holds its actual records. The domain's owner, or their DNS provider, runs it.

In practice the recursive resolver almost always has the root and `.com` referrals cached already, because so many people ask for `.com` names. So a lookup that misses the cache usually costs one trip to the authoritative server, not three. A lookup that hits the cache costs one round trip to your resolver, and often less than a millisecond if your laptop caches too.

DNS questions usually travel over UDP (User Datagram Protocol): one packet out, one packet back, with no connection set up first. That's part of why DNS is fast when it works.

### TCP: opening a connection

Once your CLI (command-line program) has an address, it opens a connection with TCP, the Transmission Control Protocol. The internet only moves individual packets, and it can lose, duplicate or reorder them. TCP turns those packets into a reliable, ordered stream of bytes between two programs.

Before any data flows, the two sides agree to talk in a **three-way handshake**:

1. Your side sends **SYN** (synchronise): "I'd like to connect, and here is the number I'll start counting my bytes from."
2. The server replies **SYN-ACK** (synchronise, acknowledge): "Agreed, and here is my starting number."
3. Your side sends **ACK**: "Got yours."

Your side can send data as soon as the SYN-ACK arrives, so connecting costs one round trip. That makes your connect time a handy measure of how far away the server is.

### TLS: making the connection private

TLS (Transport Layer Security) is the S in HTTPS. It encrypts the connection, and it proves the server really is `example.com` and not someone in between.

The proof is a **certificate**: a small file that ties a name to a public key and is signed by a certificate authority (CA), an organisation your operating system already trusts. In practice the CA's trusted root signs an intermediate certificate, and the intermediate signs the site's, so the server sends its own certificate plus the intermediates (the chain), and your side follows the chain up to a root it trusts. Every certificate has an expiry date. After it passes, clients refuse to connect.

In TLS 1.3, the current version, the handshake takes one round trip. Your side sends a **ClientHello**: which encryption methods it supports, its half of a key exchange, and the name it wants. That name is called SNI (Server Name Indication), and it lets one address serve many sites. The server answers with a **ServerHello**, its certificate, and a **Finished** message. Your side checks the certificate, sends its own Finished, and can send the request straight away. The older TLS 1.2 needs two round trips.

The ClientHello and the ServerHello travel unencrypted, because the two sides don't share a key yet. So anyone on the network path can read the SNI name and see which site you asked for, even though everything after the ServerHello is private.

- `Certificate` is the server sending its certificate chain.
- `CERT verify` is the server's CertificateVerify message: a signature that proves it holds the private key for that certificate.

### 1. Meet httptrace

Go's `net/http/httptrace` package lets you attach a `ClientTrace` to a request. A `ClientTrace` is a struct of functions, often called hooks, that the HTTP client calls as it reaches each step: `DNSStart` and `DNSDone`, `ConnectStart` and `ConnectDone`, `TLSHandshakeStart` and `TLSHandshakeDone`, and `GotFirstResponseByte`, among others.

Each step has a start hook and a done hook. Note the time when one starts and work out how long it took when it's done, and you have your timings.

### 2. Define what a check returns

A `time.Duration` is Go's type for a length of time. Zero means the step didn't happen. That's normal for DNS when the URL holds an IP address instead of a name, and for TLS on a plain `http://` URL.

`client` is a package-level variable so the tests can swap in a client that trusts their test server's certificate. Your code always uses `http.DefaultClient`.

### 3. Add the helpers that record the times

Steps 3, 4 and 5 are three consecutive parts of one function, `Check`. This step opens it, step 4 continues it, and step 5 finishes it with the closing brace.

<CodeFrom file="go/week04/trace.go" region="helpers" lang="go" title="trace.go" />

`begin`, `end` and `result` are closures: small functions defined inside `Check` that can read and change its local variables. `begin` stores the current time in one of the start variables. `end` works out how long it's been since a start time and stores that in `t`. `result` returns a copy of `t`.

Why not just write `dnsStart = time.Now()` in each hook? Because of a **data race**, and it helps to know what one is.

The HTTP client runs some of its work, such as opening the connection, in a separate goroutine: a function running at the same time as the rest of your program. (Week 5 covers goroutines properly.) The hooks run in that goroutine. Usually that's fine, because `Check` waits for the request to finish. But when the timeout runs out, `Check` returns straight away while the connection attempt carries on in the background. A moment later that goroutine calls `ConnectDone`, which writes to `t`. If `Check` is reading `t` to return it at that same moment, two goroutines are touching the same memory at once, and one of them is writing. Go makes no promise about what either side sees.

A `sync.Mutex` is a lock that only one goroutine can hold at a time. `begin`, `end` and `result` each take the lock before they touch the shared variables, so the reads and writes take turns.

You don't have to take this on trust. Delete `mu` and every `Lock` and `Unlock` call, then run `go test -race ./...`. The race detector fails `TestCheckHonorsContext` and shows both sides: a write from `end`, called by `ConnectDone` in the goroutine that opens the connection, and a read of the same memory as `Check` returns.

### 5. Send the request

<CodeFrom file="go/week04/trace.go" region="request" lang="go" title="trace.go" />

`httptrace.WithClientTrace` attaches your hooks to the request's context. A context carries a deadline and a way to cancel; when the caller's deadline passes, the request stops. (Week 5 covers contexts too.)

Three lines here matter for a monitor:

- `req.Close = true` opens a new connection for every check. Without it, Go keeps the connection open and reuses it next time, which skips DNS, connect and TLS, and your table would show them as zero.
- `CheckRedirect` stops Go from following redirects. If `http://` redirects to `https://`, you want to see the redirect status (301 or 302), not timings that mix two servers together.
- Reading the whole body means `Total` covers the full download, not just the headers.

### 8. Run it

From the folder that holds your `go.mod`, run the CLI. (In this site's examples the same command is `go run ./week04/cmd/check`, run from `examples/go`.)

```text
$ go run ./cmd/check -url https://example.com
status        200 OK
DNS          14.2 ms
connect      11.8 ms
TLS          13.5 ms
first byte   52.6 ms
total        53.1 ms
```

Read it top to bottom:

- **Connect** is one round trip, so this machine is about 12 ms from example.com's nearest server.
- **TLS** is one more round trip plus a little work for the encryption.
- **First byte** is all the rows above (39.5 ms), plus one round trip for the request and response (about 12 ms), plus the server's own work: about 1 ms here.
- **Total** is first byte plus the time to download the rest of the page, which is tiny.

Run it again straight away. Go doesn't cache DNS answers itself, so what happens next depends on your operating system. If it caches them (macOS does, and so do Linux systems running systemd-resolved), DNS drops to a millisecond or less. If not, the lookup still goes to your recursive resolver, which now has the answer cached, so it costs about one round trip to the resolver. The other rows stay about the same, because each run opens a new connection.

## Observe it

<ObserveIt>

Add one log line per check, with every timing as a `key=value` pair. Write it to standard output with Go's `log` package: call `log.SetOutput(os.Stdout)` once at start-up, then `log.Printf` once per check. The `log` package adds the date and time at the front. That format is easy to search with `grep` now, and easy for tools to read later. (Week 13 replaces it with structured logging and metrics.)

```text wrap
2026/10/10 14:02:07 check url=https://example.com status=200 dns_ms=0.8 connect_ms=11.9 tls_ms=13.2 first_byte_ms=39.6
```

Let it run against a few sites for a while, then look for these patterns. Connect time is your yardstick: it's one round trip.

- **DNS is high on the first check and low after that.** That's a cache warming up: your operating system's, if it keeps one, or your recursive resolver's. It's normal.
- **DNS is high on every check, or jumps around.** Your resolver is slow or far away. Compare it with a public one: `dig @1.1.1.1 example.com` prints a `Query time` (the side lab introduces `dig`).
- **TLS is about twice connect.** With TLS 1.3 it should be close to connect: one round trip plus a little encryption work. About twice connect usually means the server still uses TLS 1.2, which takes two round trips.
- **TLS is well over twice connect.** The server is slow at handshakes: it's overloaded, or it sends a long chain of certificates.
- **Connect is about 1,000 ms more than usual.** A packet was lost, and TCP waited a full second before sending the SYN again.
- **First byte is high, but connect and TLS look normal.** The network is fine. The app is slow.

</ObserveIt>

## Operate it

<OperateIt>

Here's what three common failures look like in your table. Try each one yourself.

**An expired certificate.** `expired.badssl.com` is a test site whose certificate expired in 2015.

```text
$ go run ./cmd/check -url https://expired.badssl.com/
status             -
DNS          18.3 ms
connect      82.4 ms
TLS          85.1 ms
first byte         -
total              -
check failed: Get "https://expired.badssl.com/": tls: failed to verify certificate: x509: certificate has expired or is not yet valid: current time 2026-10-10T14:20:31+01:00 is after 2015-04-12T23:59:59Z
exit status 1
```

The last lines are long, so scroll the box to read them. (`exit status 1` is `go run` reporting that the program exited with an error.) The key part is `x509: certificate has expired or is not yet valid`. TLS has a time, so the handshake ran, and it failed at the certificate check. (X.509, written `x509` in the error, is the standard format for certificates.) The network is fine; someone forgot to renew. Your clock can cause this too: the check compares the expiry date with your computer's time, which is why Time and NTP (Network Time Protocol) is on this week's reading list.

**A name that doesn't exist.** DNS answers NXDOMAIN (non-existent domain).

```text
$ go run ./cmd/check -url https://no-such-host.example
status             -
DNS          21.7 ms
connect            -
TLS                -
first byte         -
total              -
check failed: Get "https://no-such-host.example": dial tcp: lookup no-such-host.example: no such host
exit status 1
```

Only DNS has a time, and the error ends with `no such host`. The lookup worked, and the answer was "no such name". Look for a typo, a domain that has expired, or a DNS record someone deleted. On Linux the message also names the resolver that answered, for example `on 127.0.0.53:53`.

**A port that never answers.** A firewall that silently drops packets, instead of rejecting them, is called a black hole. `192.0.2.1` is reserved for documentation, so on most networks nothing answers it.

```text
$ go run ./cmd/check -url https://192.0.2.1 -timeout 3s
status             -
DNS                -
connect            -
TLS                -
first byte         -
total              -
check failed: Get "https://192.0.2.1": context deadline exceeded
exit status 1
```

DNS shows `-` because the URL is already an address. Connect shows `-` too: nothing ever answered the SYN, so the connection attempt never finished, and the command gave up when the full 3-second timeout ran out. Without a timeout of your own, a black hole holds a check until something else gives up: Go's default client waits 30 seconds to connect, and many other tools wait two minutes or more. (Some networks reply "no route to host" for this address straight away instead. That's a different failure, and worth recognising too.)

Compare that with a port that refuses. Port 1 on your own machine almost certainly has nothing listening:

```text
$ go run ./cmd/check -url http://127.0.0.1:1/
status             -
DNS                -
connect       0.4 ms
TLS                -
first byte         -
total              -
check failed: Get "http://127.0.0.1:1/": dial tcp 127.0.0.1:1: connect: connection refused
exit status 1
```

This time Connect has a time, because the attempt got an answer straight away. The machine is up, and it replied to the SYN with a reset: "nothing is listening here". That's the difference to look for. A refused port answers at once, so Connect has a time. A black hole never answers, so Connect stays `-` and the timeout fires.

| What you see | Most likely cause |
| --- | --- |
| TLS has a time, then a certificate error | Certificate expired, issued for another name, or not trusted |
| Only DNS has a time, then `no such host` | The name doesn't exist (NXDOMAIN) |
| Connect is `-` and the full timeout passed | Black hole: a firewall drops the packets, or the host is gone |
| Connect has a small time, then `connection refused` at once | The host is up, but nothing listens on that port |

</OperateIt>

## Side lab

<SideLab title="Trace a lookup with dig +trace and compare it with your CLI's DNS time">

`dig` is a command-line DNS client. It's already on macOS. On Ubuntu, install it with `sudo apt install dnsutils`.

**1. Ask your resolver.** A plain `dig` asks your recursive resolver, like your CLI does. This output is trimmed slightly:

```text
$ dig example.com

; <<>> DiG 9.18.30 <<>> example.com
;; global options: +cmd
;; Got answer:
;; ->>HEADER<<- opcode: QUERY, status: NOERROR, id: 31744
;; flags: qr rd ra; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 1, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1

;; QUESTION SECTION:
;example.com.			IN	A

;; ANSWER SECTION:
example.com.		3291	IN	A	203.0.113.10

;; Query time: 23 msec
;; SERVER: 192.168.1.1#53(192.168.1.1) (UDP)
;; MSG SIZE  rcvd: 56
```

`status: NOERROR` means the name exists. Try `dig no-such-host.example` and you'll see `status: NXDOMAIN`. The answer line reads: name, TTL in seconds, class (`IN` for internet), type, value. Run `dig example.com` again and watch the TTL count down. That's your resolver's cache, and the TTL is how long it has left.

**2. Do the resolver's job yourself.** `dig +trace` starts at the root and follows each referral, just like the diagram. This output is trimmed: the full version lists all 13 root servers and adds DNSSEC (DNS Security Extensions) signature records you can ignore for now.

```text
$ dig +trace example.com

.			510207	IN	NS	a.root-servers.net.
.			510207	IN	NS	b.root-servers.net.
;; Received 525 bytes from 192.168.1.1#53(192.168.1.1) in 12 ms

com.			172800	IN	NS	a.gtld-servers.net.
com.			172800	IN	NS	b.gtld-servers.net.
;; Received 1170 bytes from 198.41.0.4#53(a.root-servers.net) in 28 ms

example.com.		172800	IN	NS	a.iana-servers.net.
example.com.		172800	IN	NS	b.iana-servers.net.
;; Received 330 bytes from 192.5.6.30#53(a.gtld-servers.net) in 36 ms

example.com.		3600	IN	A	203.0.113.10
;; Received 355 bytes from 199.43.135.53#53(a.iana-servers.net) in 89 ms
```

An `NS` (name server) record names a server responsible for a zone. Read each block's last line to see who answered. The first block is your own resolver handing over the list of root servers. The next three are the root, the `.com` servers and example.com's authoritative server, one hop each, as in the diagram. Notice the final TTL is a full 3600: it came straight from the source, not from a cache.

If `+trace` stalls on the second block, your network only lets DNS through to its own resolver. Try it from home or a phone hotspot.

**3. Compare.** Add up the hops in your trace (165 ms above). Now pick a site you haven't visited today and run your CLI twice:

- The first DNS time is a real lookup through your resolver. It's usually much less than the trace total, because your resolver already had the root and `.com` referrals cached.
- The second DNS time is a cache hit: a millisecond or less if your operating system caches DNS, otherwise about one round trip to your resolver.

Write the three numbers down. Can you explain the gap between each pair?
