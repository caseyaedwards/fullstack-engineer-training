Your monitor now runs on a real domain, scales under load and survives a rollback, as <a href={weekHref(17)}>Week 17</a> left it. One question remains open: how reliable does it have to be, and how do you know when it is not? **Site reliability engineering** (SRE) answers it with numbers. ## Concepts

### SLIs, SLOs and Error Budgets

A **service level indicator** (SLI) is a number that says how well the service works for its users, written as a ratio: good events divided by valid events. A **valid event** is one you count at all, such as a `GET` of the status page. A **good event** is a valid event that went well, such as one the api answers without a server error.

A **service level objective** (SLO) is a target for an SLI over a window of time: 99.5% of status page requests succeed, measured over the last 30 days. The window rolls: every minute, the oldest minute drops out.

The **error budget** is what the target allows to fail: 100% minus the SLO. A 99.5% SLO has a budget of 0.5%. Over 30 days, 0.5% of the time is 0.005 × 30 × 24 hours = 3.6 hours, and 0.5% of 172,800 requests is 864 failed requests.

The budget turns reliability into a decision. While budget is left, you ship features, deploy on a Friday, try the risky migration. When the budget is spent, reliability work comes first until it recovers. A target of 100% leaves no budget: every deploy, every Postgres restart and every reboot of the VPS breaks it. Pick a target that the setup can meet: one VPS, one Postgres, one person who sleeps.

### Two SLOs for Your Monitor

Your monitor does two jobs for its users: it shows a status page, and it checks their sites on schedule. Each job gets one SLO.

| SLO | SLI | Target | Budget in 30 days |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Status page availability | `GET` requests to `/api/` routes that the api answers with a status under 500 | 99.5% | 0.5% of requests |
| Check freshness | 30-second steps in which checks run on schedule | 99% | 7 hours and 12 minutes |

The availability SLI counts **requests**: `http_requests_total` already carries the route and the status code of each one. A `404` for a monitor that does not exist and a `429` from the rate limit of Week 17 count as good: the api did its job. A `GET` that matches no route carries `route="unmatched"` and stays out of the SLI: no status page sends it.

The freshness SLI counts **time**, because no request asks whether a check ran on time. Every 30 seconds, Prometheus records 1 when checks run on schedule and 0 when they do not. On schedule means two things. First, the oldest job in the queue has waited 50 seconds or less: a due monitor waits up to 5 seconds for the next scheduler round, and a check takes up to 5 seconds, the worker `-timeout`, which keeps every monitor checked within its interval plus 60 seconds. Second, at least one check has finished in the last 2 minutes. Missing data counts as late.

A status page that nobody opens sends no requests, and a ratio of zero requests says nothing. A **probe** fixes that: **blackbox exporter**, a small Prometheus program, loads the status page every 15 seconds the way a browser does, through the web pod. Each probe is one more valid event, 4 a minute, 172,800 in 30 days. It also sees what the api cannot see.

### Burn Rate

The **burn rate** is how fast an SLO spends its budget, compared with the rate that spends it in exactly 30 days. At a burn rate of 1, the budget lasts the 30 days. At 2, it lasts 15. As a formula: burn rate = error ratio ÷ budget. A 3.6% error ratio against a 0.5% budget is a burn rate of 7.2.

| Burn rate | Budget lasts | Share of the 30-day budget spent in one hour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 30 days | 0.14% |
| 6 | 5 days | 0.83% |
| 14.4 | 50 hours | 2% |
| 200 | 3.6 hours | 28% |

For the availability SLO, 200 is the highest burn rate there is: every request fails, an error ratio of 1 against a budget of 0.005.

### Burn-Rate Alerts on Two Windows

The obvious alert, error ratio above 0.5% over 5 minutes, pages you for a two-minute blip that cost a sliver of the budget. Stretch the window to 3 days, and a total outage takes hours to cross the line. The Google SRE workbook, in its chapter Alerting on SLOs, combines three ideas:

- **Alert on the burn rate, not on the error ratio.** A page fires when the budget burns fast enough to matter: 14.4 over one hour spends 2% of the budget, 6 over six hours spends 5%. Both are worth an interruption. A burn rate of 1 over 3 days spends 10%: worth a ticket, not a page.
- **Use a long window** to measure the size of the problem. A two-minute blip barely moves a one-hour window.
- **Add a short window**, 1/12 of the long one, and require both. The long window keeps reading high for up to an hour after a fix; the short window drops back within minutes, which resolves the alert soon after you fix the cause.

The workbook gives these parameters for a 99.9% SLO. They depend on the share of the budget spent, not on the target, which makes them fit both of your SLOs:

| Severity | Long window | Short window | Burn rate | Budget spent when it fires |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Page | 1 hour | 5 minutes | 14.4 | 2% |
| Page | 6 hours | 30 minutes | 6 | 5% |
| Ticket | 3 days | 6 hours | 1 | 10% |

Budget spent = burn rate × long window ÷ 30 days: 14.4 × 1 hour ÷ 720 hours = 2%. For availability, a burn rate of 14.4 is an error ratio of 14.4 × 0.005 = 0.072. During a total outage the error ratio is 1, and the one-hour window reaches 0.072 after 0.072 × 60 minutes = 4.3 minutes: that is how long a full outage takes to page you. At 10% errors, it takes 43 minutes. At 5% errors the fast-burn page never fires, and the slow-burn page catches it: 6 × 0.005 = 0.03, reached after 0.03 ÷ 0.05 × 6 hours = 3.6 hours.

### Pages, Tickets and the Watchdog

A **page** interrupts you: it rings your phone and asks for action now. A **ticket** asks for action within a working day. Every alert must be one or the other and must name a runbook: a page you cannot act on teaches you to ignore pages.

Alerts run on your monitoring, and your monitoring runs on the same VPS as the app. When the VPS dies, nothing is left to page you. A **dead man's switch** turns silence into a signal: an alert that always fires sends a heartbeat to a service outside your VPS, and that service alerts you when the heartbeat stops. This page uses healthchecks.io, which has a free plan with email alerts.

### Step 9: Run the Incident Drill

A **drill** is an incident you start on purpose, at a time you choose, to test the alerts, the runbook and yourself before a real incident does. Pick a time inside your on-call hours, open a copy of the postmortem template, and write each time down as you go. Then stop Postgres on the live box:

```sh
kubectl -n your-monitor scale statefulset postgres --replicas=0
```

Postgres shuts down cleanly, and its volume stays. The api keeps answering, with `500` for every read. The workers cannot claim jobs, and the scheduler rounds fail. Now play the responder: when the page rings, open the runbook link and follow it. With 6 hours of history, a total outage needs 0.03 × 360 = 10.8 minutes to fire the slow-burn page for availability, and with 3 days, the ticket needs 0.005 × 4,320 = 21.6 minutes. On a box you deployed this week, expect the order of this test run. The tickets stayed open after the last resolve, until the outage leaves the 6-hour window, and they arrived without a sound: a ticket asks for the postmortem, not for action now.

Runbook step 5 finds the cause: `kubectl -n your-monitor get statefulset postgres` shows `0/1`. Mitigate with the runbook line:

```sh
kubectl -n your-monitor scale statefulset postgres --replicas=1
kubectl -n your-monitor rollout status statefulset postgres
```

The api, the scheduler and the workers reconnect on their next query, with no restart. Watch the alerts resolve on `localhost:9093` and on your phone. A resolve message arrives at the next `group_interval` of its group, up to 5 minutes after the alert ends. Then read what the drill cost in `slo:error_budget:remaining`. With 30 days of history, 11 minutes of total outage cost 11 ÷ 216 = 5% of the availability budget. With less history, the 30-day window holds only what Prometheus has, and the number drops far lower: the test run read −19 for availability: 20 budgets spent.

### Step 10: Write the Postmortem

A **postmortem** is the written record of an incident: what happened, why, and what changes. **Blameless** means it looks for the conditions that made the failure possible, never for a person to blame. A person who fears blame hides what they did, and the next incident has the same cause. ## Operate It

<OperateIt>

**The VPS goes down.** Nothing on the VPS can page you. When the pings stop, healthchecks.io waits out the 5-minute period and the 10-minute grace time, then emails you. This is the one alert that does not depend on your own stack.

**Telegram refuses the message.** Someone revoked the token in BotFather, or the Secret holds a typo or misses a key. Alertmanager logs one line per failed try, and retries. With a missing file:

```txt wrap
level=WARN source=retry_stage.go:165 msg="Notify attempt failed, will retry later" component=dispatcher receiver=ticket integration=telegram[0] attempts=1 err="could not read /etc/alertmanager/secrets/telegram-bot-token: open /etc/alertmanager/secrets/telegram-bot-token: no such file or directory"
```

`alertmanager_notifications_failed_total{integration="telegram"}` grows with every failed try, whatever the reason. The Watchdog still pings: the heartbeat proves that Alertmanager runs, not that pages arrive. Send a test page with `amtool` after every change to the receivers.

**A rule names a metric that does not exist.** The rule evaluates to nothing, without an error, and its alert never fires. `promtool check rules` passes. Only the unit tests and `slo_test.go` catch it, which is why both exist.

**You plan an outage.** An upgrade of Postgres takes 10 minutes. Silence the pages before you start, with a comment and an end time:

```sh
amtool --alertmanager.url=http://127.0.0.1:9093 silence add 'slo="availability"' --duration=1h --comment="Postgres upgrade"
```

A silence hides the alert, not the cost: the budget still pays for the 10 minutes. Expire the silence with `amtool silence expire <ID>` when the work ends. On the VPS, silences live in an `emptyDir`, and a new Alertmanager pod starts with none.

**Alerts flap.** A page fires, resolves and fires again every few minutes, because the burn rate sits on the threshold. Fix the cause. Raising the threshold to stop the noise also hides the next real outage.

| What you see | What it points to |
| --- | --- |
| Heartbeat email, no page | The VPS, Prometheus or Alertmanager is down |
| `Notify attempt failed` in the Alertmanager log | Wrong or missing token or chat ID, or no route to Telegram |
| An alert that never fires in a drill | A rule that names a missing metric or label |
| `ContainerCreating` on the Alertmanager pod | The `alertmanager-secrets` Secret does not exist |
| `StatusPageDown` with no `ErrorBudgetFastBurn` | Requests never reach the api: web, the api pods, or blackbox exporter |
| A page at 07:00 for something that broke at 02:00 | The on-call hours held it back, as `docs/oncall.md` promises |

</OperateIt>
