Your monitor already tells you that a site is slow. This week, you teach it to show _where_ the time goes.

Four things happen in order between your code asking for `https://example.com` and the first byte of the answer arriving. A DNS (Domain Name System) lookup turns the name into an IP (Internet Protocol) address. A TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) handshake opens a connection. A TLS (Transport Layer Security) handshake makes that connection private. Then the server does its work and replies. This week, you learn what each step does and make your monitor time every one of them.

## Goals

- Explain how a name such as `example.com` becomes an IP address, hop by hop.
- Explain what the TCP and TLS handshakes do, and why each one costs a round trip.
- Time DNS, connect, TLS and first byte on every check, with the Go `net/http/httptrace` package.
- Read those timings to tell where a slow or failing request gets stuck.

## Concepts

### Addresses, Ports and Round Trips

Machines on the internet find each other by **IP address**. An IPv4 address looks like `203.0.113.10`. The newer IPv6 kind looks like `2001:db8::10`. A **port** picks out one program on that machine. Web servers listen on port `80` for HTTP and on port `443` for HTTPS, the secure version of HTTP.

A URL such as `https://example.com/` gives you the port, through the `https` at its start, but not the address. Finding the address is the first job.

One more idea runs through this whole week. A **round trip** is one message sent and its reply received. The time it takes, the **round-trip time** or RTT, depends mostly on distance: a few milliseconds (ms) across a city, 70 to 150 ms across an ocean. Most of what you measure this week is a count of round trips.

:::note
The `curl`, `dig` and CLI outputs on this page are representative examples, not captures of one real run. Their addresses come from `203.0.113.0/24`, a range reserved for documentation. When you run the commands yourself, you see real addresses, different times and a few extra lines.
:::

### DNS: From a Name to an Address

DNS is a database spread over thousands of servers that maps names to records. The record you need most is the **A record**, which holds an IPv4 address. An **AAAA record** holds an IPv6 address.

You read a DNS name from right to left, as a path down a tree. At the top is the root, written as a dot. Below it are the top-level domains (TLDs) such as `com` and `org`. Below `com` is `example`. A different organization runs each level. Each level knows only who is responsible for the level below it. That is why a lookup takes several hops when no cache holds the answer.

Five roles take part:

- The **stub resolver** is the small DNS client built into your operating system, or into Go. It asks one question and waits for a complete answer.
- The **recursive resolver** does the legwork for the stub. Your network settings name it: your home router, a server run by your internet provider, or a public resolver such as `1.1.1.1` or `8.8.8.8`. It remembers every answer it gets, which is called caching.
- The **root servers** know where to find the server for every TLD.
- The **TLD servers** for `.com` know which servers hold the records for each `.com` domain.
- The **authoritative server** for `example.com` holds the actual records of the domain. The owner of the domain, or their DNS provider, runs it.

In practice, the recursive resolver has the root and `.com` referrals in its cache already: many people ask for `.com` names, and the `.com` referral stays valid for two days. With those referrals cached, a lookup that misses the cache for the name itself costs one trip to the authoritative server, not three. A lookup that hits the cache of the resolver costs one round trip to your resolver. When your laptop also caches answers, a repeat lookup costs a millisecond or less.

DNS questions travel over UDP (User Datagram Protocol): one packet out, one packet back, with no connection set up first. Only an answer too large for one UDP packet makes the resolver ask again over TCP. Using UDP is part of why DNS is fast when it works.

### TCP: Opening a Connection

Once your CLI (command-line program) has an address, it opens a connection with TCP. The internet moves only individual packets, and it can lose, duplicate or reorder them. TCP turns those packets into a reliable, ordered stream of bytes between two programs.

Before any data flows, the two sides agree to talk in a **three-way handshake**:

1. Your side sends **SYN**, short for synchronize: a request to connect, with the number your side starts counting its bytes from.
2. The server replies with **SYN-ACK**, short for synchronize-acknowledge: it agrees, and it sends its own starting number.
3. Your side sends **ACK**, short for acknowledge: it confirms the starting number of the server.

Your side can send data as soon as the SYN-ACK arrives. Connecting therefore costs one round trip, which makes your connect time a handy measure of how far away the server is.

### TLS: Making the Connection Private

TLS is the S in HTTPS. It encrypts the connection, and it proves that the server really is `example.com` and not someone in between.

The proof is a **certificate**: a small file that ties a name to a public key. A certificate authority (CA), an organization your operating system already trusts, signs it. In practice, the trusted root certificate of the CA signs an intermediate certificate. The intermediate signs the certificate of the site. The server therefore sends its own certificate plus the intermediates, together called the chain. Your side follows the chain up to a root it trusts. Every certificate has an expiry date. After that date passes, clients refuse to connect.

In TLS 1.3, the current version, the handshake takes one round trip. Your side sends a **ClientHello**: the encryption methods it supports, its half of a key exchange, and the name it wants. That name is called SNI (Server Name Indication), and it allows one address to serve many sites. The server answers with a **ServerHello**, its certificate, and a **Finished** message. Your side checks the certificate, sends its own Finished, and can send the request straight away. The older TLS 1.2 needs two round trips.

The ClientHello and the ServerHello travel unencrypted, because the two sides do not share a key yet. As a result, anyone on the network path can read the SNI name and see which site you ask for, even though everything after the ServerHello is private.

### Step 1: Meet httptrace

With the Go `net/http/httptrace` package, you attach a `ClientTrace` to a request. A `ClientTrace` is a struct of functions, called hooks, that the HTTP client calls as it reaches each step: `DNSStart` and `DNSDone`, `ConnectStart` and `ConnectDone`, `TLSHandshakeStart` and `TLSHandshakeDone`, and `GotFirstResponseByte`, among others.

Each step has a start hook and a done hook. Note the time in the start hook, work out the duration in the done hook, and you have your timings.

### Step 2: Define What a Check Returns

<CodeFrom file="go/week04/trace.go" region="types" lang="go" title="trace.go" />

A `time.Duration` is the Go type for a length of time. Zero means the step does not happen in that check. A zero is normal for DNS when the URL holds an IP address instead of a name, and for TLS on a plain `http://` URL.

`client` is a package-level variable, which allows the tests to swap in a client that trusts the certificate of their test server. Your code always uses `http.DefaultClient`.

### Step 3: Add the Helpers That Record the Times

Steps 3, 4 and 5 are three consecutive parts of one function, `Check`. This step opens the function. Step 4 continues it. Step 5 finishes it with the closing brace.

<CodeFrom file="go/week04/trace.go" region="helpers" lang="go" title="trace.go" />

`begin`, `end` and `result` are closures: small functions defined inside `Check` that read and change its local variables:

- `begin`: stores the current time in one of the start variables.
- `end`: works out the time elapsed since a start time and stores it in `t`.
- `result`: returns a copy of `t`.

Why not write `dnsStart = time.Now()` directly in each hook? Because that causes a **data race**.

The HTTP client runs some of its work, such as opening the connection, in a separate goroutine: a function that runs at the same time as the rest of your program. Week 5 covers goroutines in depth. The hooks run in that goroutine. While the request runs to the end, this causes no problem, because `Check` waits for the request to finish. When the timeout runs out, however, `Check` returns straight away while the connection attempt carries on in the background. A moment later, that goroutine calls `ConnectDone`, which writes to `t`. If `Check` reads `t` to return it at that same moment, two goroutines touch the same memory at once, one of them writing. Go makes no promise about what either side sees.

A `sync.Mutex` is a lock that one goroutine at a time can hold. `begin`, `end` and `result` each take the lock before they touch the shared variables. The reads and writes therefore take turns.

Test this yourself. Delete `mu` and every `Lock` and `Unlock` call, then run `go test -race ./...`. The race detector fails `TestCheckHonorsContext` and shows both sides: a write from `end`, called by `ConnectDone` in the goroutine that opens the connection, and a read of the same memory as `Check` returns.

### Step 5: Send the Request

<CodeFrom file="go/week04/trace.go" region="request" lang="go" title="trace.go" />

`httptrace.WithClientTrace` attaches your hooks to the context of the request. A context carries a deadline and a way to cancel. When the deadline of the caller passes, the request stops. Week 5 covers contexts too.

Three lines here matter for a monitor:

- `req.Close = true`: opens a new connection for every check. Without it, Go keeps the connection open and reuses it next time. The reuse skips DNS, connect and TLS. Your table then shows them as zero.

- Reading the whole body: makes `Total` cover the full download, not only the headers.

### Step 8: Run It

From the folder that holds your `go.mod`, run the CLI. In the examples of this site, the same command is `go run ./week04/cmd/check`, run from `examples/go`.

```txt
$ go run ./cmd/check -url https://example.com
status        200 OK
DNS          14.2 ms
connect      11.8 ms
TLS          13.5 ms
first byte   52.6 ms
total        53.1 ms
```

Read it from top to bottom:

- **Connect**: one round trip. This machine is therefore about 12 ms from the nearest server of `example.com`.
- **TLS**: one more round trip, plus a little work for the encryption.
- **First byte**: the 39.5 ms of the rows above, plus about 12 ms for the round trip of the request and response, plus about 1 ms of work on the server.
- **Total**: first byte plus the time to download the rest of the page, which is tiny here: 0.5 ms.

Run it again straight away. Go does not cache DNS answers itself. The second DNS time therefore depends on your operating system:

- On macOS, and on Linux systems that run `systemd-resolved`, the operating system caches the answer. DNS drops to a millisecond or less.
- On a system without a DNS cache, the lookup still goes to your recursive resolver. The resolver now has the answer cached, and the lookup costs about one round trip to the resolver.

The other rows stay about the same, because each run opens a new connection.

### Step 9: Wire It Into Your Monitor

This step is the milestone of the week: every check your monitor runs records DNS, connect, TLS and first-byte time. In the Week 3 monitor, find the loop that checks each URL, then make these changes:

- Import your `probe` package.
- Replace the request, whether it is `http.Get(url)` or `client.Get(url)`, with `probe.Check(ctx, url)`. Give each check its own deadline the way the CLI does: `context.WithTimeout` before the call, `cancel()` after it.
- Delete the timer around the old request: `start := time.Now()` and `time.Since(start)`. `Timings.Total` replaces it.
- Delete your `resp.Body.Close()`: `Check` reads and closes the body itself.
- Keep the status code and the error as before, and keep the `Timings` next to them, for example in a `Timings probe.Timings` field on your check result. You log them in the next section.

## Observe It

<ObserveIt>

Add one log line per check, with every timing as a `key=value` pair. Write it to standard output with the Go `log` package: call `log.SetOutput(os.Stdout)` once at start-up, then `log.Printf` once per check. The `log` package adds the date and time at the front. That format is easy to search with `grep` now and easy for tools to read later. Week 13 replaces it with structured logging and metrics.

```txt wrap
2026/10/10 14:02:07 check url=https://example.com status=200 dns_ms=0.8 connect_ms=11.9 tls_ms=13.2 first_byte_ms=39.6
```

Let it run against three or more sites for at least 30 minutes, then look for these patterns. Connect time is your yardstick: it is one round trip.

- **DNS is high on the first check and low after that.** That pattern is a cache warming up: the cache of your operating system, when it keeps one, or the cache of your recursive resolver. This is normal.
- **DNS is high on every check, or jumps around.** Your resolver is slow or far away. Compare it with a public resolver: `dig @1.1.1.1 example.com` prints a `Query time`. The side lab introduces `dig`.
- **TLS is about twice connect.** With TLS 1.3, TLS is close to connect: one round trip plus a little encryption work. About twice connect is the pattern of a server that still uses TLS 1.2, which takes two round trips. To confirm, run `curl -v` against the site: the `SSL connection using` line names the TLS version.
- **TLS is well over twice connect.** The server is slow at handshakes: it is overloaded, or it sends a long chain of certificates.
- **Connect is about 1000 ms more than normal.** The network loses a packet of the handshake. TCP waits a full second, then sends the SYN again.
- **First byte is high, but connect and TLS look normal.** The network is fine. The app is slow.

</ObserveIt>

## Operate It

<OperateIt>

This section shows how three common failures look in your table. Try each one yourself.

**An expired certificate.** `expired.badssl.com` is a test site with a certificate that expired in 2015.

```txt
$ go run ./cmd/check -url https://expired.badssl.com/
status             -
DNS          18.3 ms
connect      82.4 ms
TLS          85.1 ms
first byte         -
total              -
check failed: Get "https://expired.badssl.com/": tls: failed to verify certificate: x509: certificate has expired or is not yet valid: current time 2026-10-10T14:20:31+01:00 is after 2015-04-12T23:59:59Z
exit status 1
```

The last lines are long: scroll the box to read them. `exit status 1` is `go run` reporting that the program exited with an error. The key part is `x509: certificate has expired or is not yet valid`. X.509, written `x509` in the error, is the standard format for certificates. TLS has a time, which shows that the handshake ran and failed at the certificate check. The network is fine. Someone forgot to renew the certificate. A wrong clock on your computer causes the same error, because the check compares the expiry date with the time on your computer. That is why Time and NTP (Network Time Protocol) is on the reading list of this week.

**A name that does not exist.** DNS answers `NXDOMAIN`, short for non-existent domain.

```txt
$ go run ./cmd/check -url https://no-such-host.example
status             -
DNS          21.7 ms
connect            -
TLS                -
first byte         -
total              -
check failed: Get "https://no-such-host.example": dial tcp: lookup no-such-host.example: no such host
exit status 1
```

Only DNS has a time. The error ends with `no such host`. The lookup itself succeeds: its answer is that no such name exists. Look for a typo, an expired domain or a DNS record that someone deleted. On Linux, the message also names the resolver that answered, for example `on 127.0.0.53:53`.

**A port that never answers.** A **black hole** is a firewall that silently drops packets instead of rejecting them. `192.0.2.1` is reserved for documentation, and no host on the internet answers it. On a network that drops packets to this address, the check meets a black hole:

```txt
$ go run ./cmd/check -url https://192.0.2.1 -timeout 3s
status             -
DNS                -
connect            -
TLS                -
first byte         -
total              -
check failed: Get "https://192.0.2.1": context deadline exceeded
exit status 1
```

DNS shows `-` because the URL is already an address. Connect shows `-` too: nothing answers the SYN. The connection attempt never finishes. The command gives up when the full 3-second timeout runs out. Without a timeout of your own, a black hole holds a check until something else gives up. `http.DefaultClient` waits 30 seconds to connect. Many other tools wait 2 minutes or more. On a network whose router rejects packets to this address, the check fails at once with `no route to host` instead. That failure is different, and worth recognizing too.

Compare that with a port that refuses. On a default macOS or Ubuntu install, no program listens on port `1` of your own machine:

```txt
$ go run ./cmd/check -url http://127.0.0.1:1/
status             -
DNS                -
connect       0.4 ms
TLS                -
first byte         -
total              -
check failed: Get "http://127.0.0.1:1/": dial tcp 127.0.0.1:1: connect: connection refused
exit status 1
```

This time, Connect has a time, because the attempt gets an answer straight away. The machine is up, and it replies to the SYN with a reset: the TCP way to say that nothing listens on that port. That reset is the difference to look for. A refused port answers at once, which gives Connect a time. A black hole never answers: Connect stays `-` until the timeout fires.

| What you see | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| TLS has a time, then a certificate error | The certificate is expired, issued for another name, or not trusted |
| Only DNS has a time, then `no such host` | The name does not exist: `NXDOMAIN` |
| Connect is `-` and the full timeout passes | Black hole: a firewall drops the packets, or the host is gone |
| Connect has a small time, then `connection refused` at once | The host is up, but nothing listens on that port |

</OperateIt>

## Side Lab

<SideLab title="Trace a lookup with dig +trace and compare it with the DNS time of your CLI">

`dig` is a command-line DNS client. It is already on macOS. On Ubuntu, install it with `sudo apt install dnsutils`.

**1. Ask your resolver.** A plain `dig` asks your recursive resolver, as your CLI does. This example omits a few lines of the output:

```txt
$ dig example.com

; <<>> DiG 9.18.30 <<>> example.com
;; global options: +cmd
;; Got answer:
;; ->>HEADER<<- opcode: QUERY, status: NOERROR, id: 31744
;; flags: qr rd ra; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 1, AUTHORITY: 0, ADDITIONAL: 1

;; QUESTION SECTION:
;example.com.			IN	A

;; ANSWER SECTION:
example.com.		3291	IN	A	203.0.113.10

;; Query time: 23 msec
;; SERVER: 192.168.1.1#53(192.168.1.1) (UDP)
;; MSG SIZE  rcvd: 56
```

`status: NOERROR` means the name exists. Run `dig no-such-host.example` to see `status: NXDOMAIN`. The answer line reads, from left to right: name, TTL in seconds, class, type and value. The class `IN` stands for internet. Run `dig example.com` again and watch the TTL count down. The countdown is the cache of your resolver at work. The TTL is the number of seconds the answer has left in that cache.

**2. Do the job of the resolver yourself.** `dig +trace` starts at the root and follows each referral, as in the diagram. This example trims the output: the full version lists all 13 root servers and adds DNSSEC (DNS Security Extensions) signature records, which you can ignore for now.

```txt
$ dig +trace example.com

.			510207	IN	NS	a.root-servers.net.
.			510207	IN	NS	b.root-servers.net.
;; Received 525 bytes from 192.168.1.1#53(192.168.1.1) in 12 ms

com.			172800	IN	NS	a.gtld-servers.net.
com.			172800	IN	NS	b.gtld-servers.net.
;; Received 1170 bytes from 198.41.0.4#53(a.root-servers.net) in 28 ms

example.com.		172800	IN	NS	a.iana-servers.net.
example.com.		172800	IN	NS	b.iana-servers.net.
;; Received 330 bytes from 192.5.6.30#53(a.gtld-servers.net) in 36 ms

example.com.		3600	IN	A	203.0.113.10
;; Received 355 bytes from 199.43.135.53#53(a.iana-servers.net) in 89 ms
```

An `NS` (name server) record names a server responsible for a zone. Read the last line of each block to see who answers it. The first block is your own resolver handing over the list of root servers. The next three are the root, the `.com` servers and the authoritative server of `example.com`, one hop each, as in the diagram. The final TTL is a full `3600`: the answer comes straight from the source, not from a cache.

If `+trace` stalls on the second block, your network allows DNS traffic only to its own resolver. Try it from home or from a phone hotspot.

**3. Compare.** Add up the hops in your trace: 165 ms in the example above. Now pick a site you have not visited today and run your CLI twice:

- The first DNS time is a real lookup through your resolver. When your resolver already has the root and `.com` referrals in its cache, this time is much less than the trace total.
- The second DNS time is a cache hit: a millisecond or less if your operating system caches DNS, otherwise about one round trip to your resolver.

Write the three numbers down, then explain the gap between each pair.
